## A customer and payment mechanism you can test

### Initial customer

An owner-led business that is adding people or teams, has recurring roles, and lacks dedicated learning and development staff. Industry and headcount are hypotheses to narrow through interviews.

**Buying trigger:** onboarding repeatedly consumes manager time, new hires cannot do a critical task independently, or avoidable mistakes and rework persist.

### Initial offer

**One role. One performance problem. A diagnosis and a scoped next step.**

Begin where the owner can identify a recent incident and a measurable cost. Avoid selling a complete training department before the first problem is understood.

### Step 1 — Short fit conversation

A 15–20-minute discussion confirms the problem, decision maker, timing, and access to a supervisor and workflow. This can be free; it is not a free comprehensive assessment. If the issue is staffing, equipment, incentives, or an unclear process, say so.

### Step 2 — Paid diagnostic: proposed $1,450

This is a **price hypothesis**, adapted from your historical Skis proposal—not a validated employee-development sale. Budget 10–18 hours of your effort for one role and one problem; clarify the actual scope before any engagement.

- Interview the owner, one supervisor, and one experienced employee; observe or review one workflow.
- Define the critical task and observable standard; document the current method and likely causes of the gap.
- Establish a simple baseline from available records or a short observation.
- Deliver a performance map, a recommendation on whether training is appropriate, and an implementation scope with a separate price.

Customer participation assumption: three roughly 30-minute conversations, access to a representative workflow, and a review meeting. Validate this burden with buyers. Agree deliverables, schedule, access, confidentiality, and payment terms in writing. A possible test is 50% at booking and 50% on delivery; it is a proposed term, not an existing customer agreement.

### Step 3 — Separately priced implementation

Build the job aid, practice cases, supervisor checks, and follow-up for the selected task. Use the client's existing software where feasible. Quote a fixed fee only after the work and stakeholder time are understood. Optional later support can be hourly; the $165/hour figure in your historical proposal is an anchor to test, not proof of today's market rate.

**Pricing reality:** $1,450 divided by 10–18 delivery hours is about $145–$81 per hour before sales time, overhead, revisions, taxes, and unpaid discovery. Keep the diagnostic bounded. Do not bundle a whole training framework into that fee.

### What makes the service worth buying?

The hypothesis is that the owner will pay for a better decision and a practical improvement in work: fewer coaching interruptions, faster independent performance, or less rework. AI-generated content and attractive stories alone are easy to substitute. Diagnosis, access to the real workflow, realistic practice, manager reinforcement, and measurement are the proposed value.

For the first employee-development pilot, avoid outcome bonuses until baseline definitions, measurement periods, attribution, and customer responsibilities are agreed. Your older recruiting bonus structure does not automatically transfer to this offer.

### 20-minute interview guide

1. **Start with the last real case:** “Think about the last person you hired or moved into a new role. What were they expected to do, and where did they need the most help?”
2. “Who taught them? How long before they could do the important tasks independently? What happened when they got stuck?”
3. “Give me a recent example of a mistake, rework, or repeated explanation. What did it cost in time, money, delays, or customer impact?” Ask how that estimate was derived.
4. “What have you tried—shadowing, SOPs, software, outside help? What worked? What still failed?”
5. “Have you spent money solving this before? Who approved it? What would make it urgent this month or quarter?”
6. **Only now present the offer:** “I'm exploring a focused assessment of one role and one problem, followed by a practical implementation if warranted. It would involve you and a supervisor. The initial price I'm testing is $1,450.”
7. “How would you decide whether that is worth buying? What would the assessment need to produce? What would prevent you from doing it?” Avoid treating politeness as willingness to pay.
8. “Would a next step make sense: reviewing a sample, scoping an actual problem, involving the decision maker, or none of those?” Record the action they choose.

Do not lead with “You waste lots of money on training, right?” Do not ask respondents to assess your favorite idea before understanding their actual work. Write down their language and distinguish observations, estimates, and your interpretation.

## A small demonstration you can show today

**Task:** hand off a client job to the office or next team with enough information for them to act. This is an illustrative exercise, not an actual Doxa or Skis policy. A real client must define its own standard. It demonstrates your method without requiring another software product.

**Successful performance:** the receiving person can see which job is involved, its current state, supporting evidence, the unresolved issue, the person responsible, and the next action with a due time. They acknowledge receipt or follow the company's agreed handoff process.

### 1. Worked example

*It's 3:15 p.m. Maya's crew has finished the scheduled work on Job 218. The client has requested an additional item that is not yet approved. The office needs the update before tomorrow's dispatch. Maya could say “We're done,” but that leaves a hidden decision for someone else.*

**Job:** 218 · Main Street site  
**Status:** Scheduled scope complete; requested extra item pending approval.  
**Evidence:** Completion photos saved in the job record; customer request logged.  
**Open issue:** Extra item has no authorization; do not schedule it as approved work.  
**Owner:** Office coordinator obtains approval or a decision.  
**Next action:** Confirm the decision by 10 a.m. tomorrow before dispatch; receiving coordinator acknowledges the handoff.

The story creates a reason to use the fields. The performance aid is a short template. The example shows the decision, not just terminology.

### 2. Guided practice

*Job 307 is almost complete. One inspection is still pending. The supervisor leaves at 4 p.m.; tomorrow's team starts at 7 a.m. You have the inspection request but no confirmed time.*

Fill the six fields. What must remain “pending”? Who needs to own the next action? What would the receiving team need to know before starting?

### Feedback guide

A useful answer identifies the job and pending inspection, links the request as evidence, states that the time is unconfirmed, assigns a named role to follow up, and sets a next-action deadline appropriate to the 7 a.m. start. Do not invent inspection approval. The client would define the actual escalation and scheduling rules.

### 3. Independent case

*Job 412 is reported complete, but the photo record is missing and a supply item has been returned without a receipt. The office will close records at noon tomorrow.*

Prepare the handoff without prompts. The reviewer checks each field, the accuracy of “complete” versus “unverified,” and whether the next person can act without chasing the sender for essential details.

### 4. Supervisor check and follow-up

Use two varied cases and the same observable standard. Record missing essential details and clarification requests. Repeat a short check about a week later during normal work. Passing a practice case alone is not proof of sustained proficiency.

### 5. Evaluate the business outcome

Before use, sample several actual handoffs and record preparation time, missing details, and clarification loops. After adoption, sample comparable work. Keep case difficulty and workflow changes in view. Avoid attributing every improvement to the lesson. Ask a supervisor and one learner whether the aid helps or adds friction.

Your Crew Bonus Excel story is another possible demonstration, especially if the target task involves a spreadsheet. Do not build a large course until interviews identify a relevant task and a buyer.
